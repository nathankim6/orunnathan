## [1~2] 다음 글을 읽고, 물음에 답하시오. (교과서 3과 — 지역별 영어의 사전 수록)

In 1828, an American lawyer and author Noah Webster published "An American Dictionary of the English Language" with a goal of providing America with its own version of the English language. (a) Most dictionaries in Webster's day focused on prescribing how English words should be used rather than documenting the way people actually used English in daily life. (b) In his dictionary, Webster challenged this convention by attempting to describe the language as it was spoken by Americans. (c) When he included informal expressions in his dictionary, he was criticized by those who believed that such words were polluting the English language. (d) Webster, however, argued that these words reflected distinctive American variations of English, helping differentiate American English from British English. (e) With the rise of English as the world's lingua franca, efforts have been made to register English variations used around the globe, one example was the creation of the Oxford Handbook of World Englishes. Now, it is essential for English dictionaries to recognize and document words and phrases that reflect the identities and experiences of the multicultural populations using English.

## [5~6] 다음 글을 읽고, 물음에 답하시오. (교과서 3과 — 영어의 지역적 적응)

When a language is adopted in a specific region or community, it is natural for it to undergo changes or modifications as the local people utilize the language to more accurately reflect their daily realities and cultural contexts. Linguistic change takes place across various aspects of language, but particularly noticeable in vocabulary. English vocabulary has been modified and new expressions have been created within the different varieties of English.

In Indian English, the term "prepone" was coined to mean "to reschedule" as the opposite of "postpone." For instance, if a meeting scheduled for Thursday is moved to Wednesday, it is considered "preponed." Hong Kong English speakers shout "add oil" to show encouragement or support, a direct translation from a Chinese expression, which originally meant to inject more gasoline into an engine. In the Philippines, there are many houses with "dirty kitchens," which are not actually "dirty" kitchens. Instead, they refer to kitchens outside the home, where most of the cooking takes place to avoid trapping heat and smell inside the house in a hot climate. In Nigerian English, heavy traffic congestion is often referred to as a "go-slow." Someone who says, "I was caught in a go-slow this morning," simply means that they were stuck in a traffic jam. In South African English, the word "robot" is commonly used to mean "traffic light." Therefore, a foreigner seeing a road sign that says "Robot Ahead" might initially expect to encounter a human-like machine, only to be surprised that a traffic sign appears instead. This evolution allows language to better match up with the everyday experiences and cultural contexts of the communities that utilize it.

## [23~25] 다음 글을 읽고, 물음에 답하시오. (NY Times 뉴스 · 다이아몬드 — De Beers 의 Lightbox 철수)

The world's biggest diamond miner, De Beers, whose very existence depends on the popularity of natural stones, is facing an existential threat?

You wouldn't think so to look at Al Cook, the jovial chief executive of De Beers, as he sat in the company's London headquarters last week. After a boom in spending during the pandemic, the natural diamond market has had a difficult few years. Alongside the acceptance of lab-grown diamonds, fewer marriages, plunging demand in China, Russian sanctions and a volatile global economy all played a part.

As part of its cost-cutting measures, De Beers confirmed this week that it would shut down Lightbox, a fashion jewelry label it introduced in 2018 to sell synthetic diamonds at a time when a synthetic price discount was only 10 percent of their natural counterparts. (A) The gambit was pitched as a bold experiment to show consumers the difference between natural and lab-grown diamonds, offering lower than market prices in a bid to protect De Beers's core business. But did it work?

"Kind of," Mr. Cook said. Lightbox, he said, was designed to sell (a) man-made diamonds at value pegged to what it cost to make them rather than metrics like carat, cut, color and clarity that guide the price of natural stones. Because of declining manufacturing costs, however, the wholesale price of (b) mass-produced alternatives has fallen by 90 percent since the unveiling of Lightbox. "The fact that you can now buy a $299 engagement ring at Walmart would be a win in the eyes of my predecessors," Mr. Cook said, adding that people didn't perceive that sort of purchase as an "heirloom," or investment.

If some consumers won't want a "luxury" item with (c) a center stone that costs almost nothing, the theory — or great hope — is that the appeal of natural stones could remain in place. But Paul Zimnisky, an independent diamond industry analyst and consultant, said it was increasingly clear that many consumers don't understand the difference and hadn't been helped by retailers seduced by the prospect of margins as high as 80 to 90 percent on (d) synthetic diamonds, compared with 20 to 40 percent for natural counterparts.

"There is still a lot of confusion among prospective buyers," he said. "Consumers have been told over and over that the two products are exactly the same, when the reality is they can be chemically distinguished with certainty." "Consumers are willing to spend ten times more for (e) a gemstone whose appeal stems from its rarity, but they need to be guaranteed that what they are buying is in fact a natural," Mr. Zimnisky said.

Given a mass consumer mind-set that bigger is definitely better, particularly among the all-important bridal market, the future for natural diamonds will come down to marketing — as it always has. But the odds are stacking up against its players. President Trump's proposed tariffs could cause the industry headaches (though Mr. Cook said he thought that in the long term diamonds may gain an exemption, and Mr. Zimnisky said that one bright spot could be a boost to the secondhand diamond market in the United States).

## [26~28] 다음 글을 읽고, 물음에 답하시오. (NY Times 뉴스 · 번개 — 번개를 견디는 열대수목)

Gora needed a more efficient way to find struck trees, so he and his collaborators developed a method for monitoring lightning strikes and triangulating their electromagnetic signals. The technique led him more quickly to the right tree, which he could assess using a drone.

From 2014 to 2019, the system captured 94 lightning strikes on trees. Gora and his team visited sites to see which species had been struck. They were looking for dead trees as well as "flashover points," where leaves are singed as lightning jumps between trees. From there, the canopy dies back, and the tree eventually dies.

Eighty-five species had been struck and seven survived, but (A) one stood out literally and figuratively: Dipteryx oleifera, a towering species that had been struck nine times, including one tree that had been hit twice and seemed more vigorous. D. oleifera stands about 30% taller than the rest of the trees and has a crown about 50% larger than others, almost as if it is an arboreal lightning rod. "It seems to have an architecture that is potentially selecting to be struck more often," Gora said.

All the struck D. oleifera trees survived lightning strikes, but 64% of other species died within two years. (a) Trees surrounding D. oleifera were 48% more likely to die after a lightning strike than those around other species. In one notable die-off, a single strike killed 57 trees around D. oleifera "while the central tree is just happy and healthy," Gora said. Lightning also blasted parasitic vines off D. oleifera trees.

(b) The clearing of neighboring trees and choking vines meant struck D. oleifera trees had more competition for light, making it easier to grow and produce more seeds. Computer models estimated that getting struck multiple times could extend the life of a D. oleifera tree by almost 300 years.

Before the study, "it seemed impossible that lightning could be a good thing for the trees," Gora said. But the evidence suggests that D. oleifera benefits from each jolt. (c) "Trees are in constant competition with each other, and you just need an edge relative to whatever is surrounding you," said Gabriel Arellano, a forest ecologist at the University of Michigan who was not involved in the study.

The physical mechanisms that help trees survive intense lightning strikes remain unknown. (d) Different trees could be more conductive or have architectures that escape damage, Gora suggested. While the study was only in Panama, similar patterns have been observed in other tropical forests. "It's remarkably common," said Adriane Esquivel Muelbert, a forest ecologist at the University of Birmingham in England who has collaborated with Gora but was not involved in the study. "It's quite clear when it happens."

Climate change is set to increase the frequency and severity of thunderstorms in the tropics. (e) Some trees, it seems, may be better equipped for a stormy future than others.
